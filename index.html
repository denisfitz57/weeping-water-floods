A history in clippings · Cass County, Nebraska

When the Creek Rose

Weeping Water Creek is a quiet stream that hardly anyone outside Cass County has heard of. A few times a generation it turns into a river. This is the story of those days, told by the people who lived through them.

Weeping Water · Nehawka · Union · Elmwood · Avoca1883 to todayDrawn from 42,000+ newspaper pages

The Otoe and Omaha people called it Nigahoe, the rustling water, for the sound it made going over the falls. French traders misheard the name, and the town that grew up on its banks became Weeping Water. The creek begins near Alvo, picks up its north and south branches around Weeping Water and Avoca, and winds past Nehawka and Union to the Missouri River. On a normal summer day you can wade across it.

The town's newspaper, the Weeping Water Republican, was published for more than a century, from 1882 until it was folded into the Plattsmouth Journal in 1986. In those pages the creek turns up again and again. Bridges float off, basements fill, carp turn up between the railroad tracks, and the same families watch the water come up to their doors. There are rescues, a drowning, a few jokes, and, from 1950 on, a long and often frustrating campaign to keep it from happening again.

The gage at Union went in during 1950, shortly after the creek's worst flood up to that time, and the first entry in its record is that flood. The USGS reported that the 1950 crest was “more than twice as large as the highest previously known flood of June 1947.” For anything earlier, the newspaper is our only gage.

1883June

Arthur Marshall Loses His Pants

The earliest flood in the record survives only because the paper reprinted it twenty years later.

The Republican's surviving issues from 1883 stop in April, so the paper's original report of the June flood is lost. In June 1903 the editor ran a “Twenty Years Ago” column reprinting it, and the story leads off with Arthur Marshall, who “was looking for his pants Monday. He lost them in Sunday's flood.”

The rest of the column is not funny. Rain came down so hard that “probably more water fell in this vicinity than ever before.” Water ran through the streets carrying boxes and barrels. At five o'clock Sunday morning people living on the low ground on both sides of the creek woke to find the water rising. The iron bridge on Randolph Street went three feet under, and it stayed put only because someone had chained it to the trees on the bank.

Henry Hogget tried to ride his horse out to the Randolph bridge, and the horse went off the high grade. Hogget couldn't swim. He caught hold of a small tree and hung on until men in a boat got to him, “but only in the nick of time, for in a moment afterwards the oil house of P. S. Barnes came floating down and struck the tree to which he had been clinging and buried it beneath the angry waters.”

The flood also produced the first relief drive in the record. Mrs. Gray and Mrs. Sewell Reed, whose homes on the bottoms filled with water, received about $50 raised by subscription. The same pattern shows up in nearly every flood that follows: the water comes, the town pitches in, and someone takes up a collection.

“The iron bridge on Randolph street was three feet under water … held in place by chains fastened to trees on the banks.”Weeping Water Republican, June 1883, reprinted June 18, 1903

1903–35Between the big ones

Teamsters, Ice Gorges and a Country Doctor

Smaller floods, a few heroes, and the one death the creek is known to have caused.

For the next half century the creek flooded every few years, and the Republican reported each one in a few lines. In June 1917 more than five inches of rain sent the creek out of its banks, “the highest it has been for a long time.” The headline was cheerful anyway: Big Rains Visit This Section and Helps Make Big Potato Crop. In 1919 a “semi-cloud-burst” washed out the bridge near the Wm. Ash place on Commercial Street.

In September 1921 the creek covered the federal highway at Union, and the teamsters there earned about $500 hauling stranded cars through the flood with horse teams at a dollar a car. After dark they raised the price, and the paper reported that “there was not one traveler who thought the price unfair.”

Two years later, on September 28, 1923, a cloudburst hit Louisville, a few miles north on Mill Creek, and killed a dozen people. Weeping Water read the news closely, and Louisville never forgot it. When storms came in later years, people along Railroad Avenue there moved to high ground, “fearing a repetition of the big flood.”

In March 1929 heavy snow melted fast, and an ice gorge formed below the Nehawka bridge. The creek “reached the highest stage ever seen by the residents of Nehawka.” Water rose to the lower seats of the ballpark grandstand, V. P. Sheldon's big red barn ended up on an island, and country children who had made it to school were put up overnight by town families. The paper said the high water “was a real curiosity to everyone who saw it.”

August 1932: Dr. Hansen

Six or seven inches of rain fell on the night of August 11, 1932. In Weeping Water the pump house flooded, and the town was without water until the following Tuesday. That Sunday night a gasoline pump at the Dowler garage caught fire, and “citizens' hearts sank, because each one knew that the water supply had run out.” The volunteer firemen put it out with chemical extinguishers.

Nehawka lost its doctor. Dr. Douglas E. Hansen was called to the Nelson Berger home early Friday morning. He found four feet of water running over the fill south of town, so he borrowed a horse and tried to ford. The horse slipped off the grade and both went into the current. Rescue parties searched a stream that had spread half a mile wide, and they found his body that afternoon, about 100 feet from where he went in. The Republican remembered him as a man who “came to Weeping Water often to play golf and mix with the boys,” and in 1987 a Journal history of Nehawka was still telling the story.

In June 1935, the week after floods on the Republican River killed more than a hundred people in western Nebraska, the creek ran “a roaring torrent” and came close to the top of its banks, but it did not go over.

1947June 4 · 11 · 18 · 22

Four Floods in One June, and an Oil Tank

Crowds came by special bus to watch a storage tank batter the town's bridges.

June of 1947 was wet all month. The creek flooded on June 4, flooded again a week later, and on June 18 Cass County “experienced floods on Weeping Water Creek yesterday for the third consecutive Wednesday.” Trains stopped running between Nehawka and Union. The Swedetown bridge went out, and some officials said the county should pay to replace it.

Then on Sunday, June 22, 4.12 inches fell on town while cloudbursts upstream near Wabash, Elmwood and Eagle sent their water down the creek as well. The Republican called it “the worst flood in the history of Weeping Water.” J. I. Corley, who had lived in town for 65 years, said he had never seen the creek so high. It knocked out the municipal water plant for the third time that month, and it left the channel through the middle of town ten feet wider than before.

On June 4 the water had carried off a Keckler Oil Co. storage tank, which came to rest against the old power house. James P. Howland waded in up to his hips and lashed it to the railroad tracks, and Richard Keckler, in swim trunks, helped. On the 22nd the tank broke loose again and floated down to the west bridge. All afternoon it beat against the bridge as the water rose and fell. Radio stations reported on it, and so many people came to watch that a special bus ran from Louisville.

“With one dramatic stroke, the current swept the tank under the bridge … Dozens of people, who had been watching at the bridge, ran up the alley towards the east bridge, expecting to see the tank sweep the bridge away.”Weeping Water Republican, June 26, 1947

It didn't. “The tank struck with a heavy thud, the bridge groaned and swayed,” and the crowd stayed until dark. By midnight the city reservoir was empty. On Monday water came into town in tin cans, and bottled drinks sold out.

1950May 8–9

The Night of May 8

The flood of record: twice the size of 1947, and the reason the town started trying to tame the creek.

60,300cubic feet per second at Union, more than twice any earlier known flood (USGS)
30,300cfs at Weeping Water itself
310Cass County bridges damaged; ten washed out completely
1life lost at Union, where several homes were destroyed

Heavy rain started late on May 8, 1950, and centered on the headwaters of Salt Creek, the Little Nemaha and Weeping Water Creek. Across southeast Nebraska the storm killed 23 people. In Weeping Water, the water peaked during the night. By Tuesday morning the east bridge into the business district was gone. The homes of Chub Rector and Lee Straton were “completely demolished and very few of their furnishings were recovered.” Neighbors held a shower for Mrs. Straton to replace what she had lost.

The library's archive has no issue for May 11, 1950, the week of the flood. Nobody now knows whether the paper missed that week or the copy was simply lost. The next issue, on May 18, ran ten pages, with an aerial photograph from Nell Munkres and a staff photo of what was left of the east bridge. In the USGS's report on the flood, the gage observer at Union noted that his house had been destroyed and that readings for May 8 were lost.

Governor Val Peterson declared Cass County an emergency area. At a meeting at the Lincoln airport, Gen. Lewis A. Pick, chief of the Army Engineers, was surprised to learn that several bridges damaged in 1947 had never been repaired, and he said that “at least two-thirds of the damage from last week's flood could have been eliminated” if earlier Missouri Valley plans had been adopted.

Flood chicks and gasoline fish

People also found things to laugh about. The Norris Hatchery thanked customers for “the wonderful response … on our FLOOD SPECIAL chicks” and kept the special prices for the rest of the season. Forty years later the Journal's anniversary column recalled two more details from that week: milk was sold in half-gallon cartons in Weeping Water for the first time, and

“Most of the fish caught east of town seem to be saturated with gasoline … if you eat these fish, you will really have gas on your stomach.”“40 Years Ago,” Plattsmouth Journal, May 24, 1990

That summer the Parker Construction Co. laid a new water main under the creek and salvaged girders and planks from the wrecked Swedetown and east bridges to build footbridges. In 1951 Henry Friesel, Albert Jacobsen and Rasmus Lauritzen built the swinging footbridge at the end of East P Street with city funds, so Swedetown children could walk to school. Friesel, who lived in Swedetown, looked after it for decades.

1950–2000Fifty years of meetings

Taming the Creek

Terraces, a watershed association, two federal rejections, a disputed plan with 58 reservoirs, and finally, in the 1990s, 19 dams.

Within weeks of the 1950 flood, the Weeping Water Community Club voted to lobby the county commissioners about the lost bridges. In January 1951 more than 150 farmers, businessmen and housewives organized the Cass-Weeping Water Creek Watersheds Association. The idea was to “hold the water on the land where it falls.” That meant terraces, contour farming and grassed waterways first, then small flood-storage dams on the tributaries, and larger dams downstream. There were already 1,600 miles of terraces in the watershed.

A cloudburst in May 1954 made the case for them. Earl Puls, northwest of Manley, got water as high as in 1950, but the terraced part of his farm came through, and the Soil Conservation Service said the rain proved “conservation's worth.”

Getting the dams built took much longer than anyone expected. In September 1982 the Republican printed the history as a timeline under the headline Watershed History Has Been One Of Floods, Project Delays:

  • 1951Watershed association formed.
  • 1954, 1964, 1965More floods. A steering committee collects damage reports from landowners.
  • 1966–67Application for federal planning help sent to the Soil Conservation Service.
  • 1969Weeping Water Creek Watershed Conservancy District formed.
  • 1972Nebraska replaces 155 small districts with 24 Natural Resources Districts; the creek falls under the Lower Platte South NRD.
  • 1973–75The project ranks 20th on the state priority list, then 6th, but only the top five go forward.
  • 1975The city adopts floodplain rules to qualify for federal flood insurance.
  • 1979 & 1981The Bureau of Reclamation, then the SCS, find the project “not feasible for funding.”
  • 1982, 1983, 1984Three floods in three years. The NRD hires an Omaha firm (HDR) to draw up a plan.
  • Nov. 1984The NRD adopts “Plan A”: 58 reservoirs at $7.9 million, projected to cut average annual flood damage by 55.6%.
  • 1987The NRD and the city split the cost of straightening a curve in the creek between the two bridges and lining the banks with riprap.
  • 1989–90The state approves 75% funding for 20 dams costing $5.2 million, the first comprehensive watershed project in Nebraska built without a federal program.
  • 1992–2000Nineteen dams built on the tributaries. Average annual flood damage is projected to fall by nearly 38%.
  • 2024The NRD helps fund design of a Weeping Water Creek Stabilization and Realignment Project, where the channel has shifted north into City Park.

The plan had its critics. Bill Whipple of Union had been pushing for flood protection since 1950, and he argued that the watershed should be split into fifteen or so small districts with small structures. A Lincoln board member objected that the dams would return “only 50 cents for every dollar spent.” Buzz Brown, writing to the editor after the creek flooded yet again in 1987, said what many people felt:

“Of course everyone gets concerned when the creek floods, but after a few days, when it dries up, forget all about it.”Letter to the editor, Plattsmouth Journal, June 11, 1987

Did the dams work? They drain only part of the watershed and were designed to reduce flooding, not to end it. The worst recorded flood, in 1993, came while they were still being built, and in 2010 the creek rose to 48,200 cfs at Union. Since 2010, every annual peak has stayed below 12,000 cfs.

1954–80The in-between years

A Canoe Trip, and Water Over the Dam

Some floods were small, and some were nearly tragic.

On Memorial Day 1962, 4.96 inches fell, the most in 24 hours since 1950. Two boys, Gary Wiles and Joe Ward, decided it was a good time to take a canoe down the creek. They put in at the Wiles farm three and a half miles west of town and got along fine until they tried to go over the dam west of the City Lakes. The canoe flipped. Joe grabbed a tree limb and got out. Gary rode the overturned canoe about a mile downstream, until Lloyd Lane, Jed Friesel and Leon Wendt lowered a rope from the footbridge near the Jim Doyle home and he got it over his shoulders as he passed under. The men said “it was all they could do to hold him.”

The creek flooded in 1964 and again in May 1965, and both times it came to the edge of the Tom Sorensen home on East Street, which comes up again later in this story. In July 1978 it topped its banks after heavy rains upstream. Water fourteen feet deep poured into the underground Kerford quarry. The new footbridge to the tennis courts had to be tied to a tree with rope. Youngsters tried to catch fish that were trapped on the tennis court, and gardeners went out in the dark to pull whatever vegetables they could save. In June 1980 it came “boiling over the dam just west of the City Park.” The photo caption added: “Every once in awhile, Mother Nature just has to let you know whose boss.”

Gary Wiles and Joe Ward, if you're out there, we'd like to hear your side of the canoe story.

1982–87Three floods in three years

Rampaging Creek Sweeps Away City Bridge

The Randolph Street bridge goes downstream, volunteers stay up all night, and carp turn up between the rails.

Shortly after 4 a.m. on June 15, 1982, the 80-foot single-lane bridge on Randolph Street buckled and went into the creek, and the current carried it 140 yards downstream into the trees. Residents of Riverside Addition, which everyone called “Swedetown,” were warned to leave. The swinging footbridge their children used to walk to school was left a tangle of cable and debris. Volunteers worked through the night moving people and furniture. Maj. Gen. Edward Binder of the Nebraska National Guard flew into the Weeping Water airport by 7:30 that morning, and Weeping Water was the first community in the 31 affected counties to apply for emergency funds.

As the water went down, the paper reported, it left “a veritable paradise for fishermen, who had a field day hand-catching many carp and a scattering of catfish which were trapped between the rails of the Missouri Pacific railroad tracks.”

The creek flooded again in June 1983. Then on June 12, 1984, 7.42 inches of rain fell on Weeping Water in nine hours. The USGS measured 53,500 cfs at Union, second only to 1950 at the time. The town siren didn't work, so emergency vehicles drove the streets to wake people. About 25 people were evacuated, firemen from Avoca and Manley helped, and the water ran over both bridges from 4:30 to 6 a.m. The pedestrian approach to the west bridge washed away and the tennis courts were ruined. Mr. and Mrs. Chester Hopper served breakfast to the volunteers at the D & B Diner. Cass County's erosion damage was estimated at $10 million, and the NRD's manager said the proposed watershed project “would certainly have reduced the damages from this flood.”

Again in May 1987 the creek rose six and a half feet above flood stage. People sandbagged, and one rescue worker broke an arm doing it. Afterward a city employee was photographed shoveling ten inches of mud off the tennis courts.

1964–87Human interest

The Sorensens of East Street

If one family's name runs through these flood stories, it is Sorensen.

The Tom Sorensen house sat on East Street, between the railroad tracks and the north bank of the creek. When the water rose, it was usually the first house in Weeping Water to be threatened, and the Republican's photographers knew where to go. Fred Ward photographed it in 1965, again in 1980, and again in 1982.

Over two decades the family moved its belongings out and back in again, lost a basement full of canned vegetables, and learned to call the fire department at five in the morning. Through all of it they kept thanking the volunteers who helped them. In 1984 Bruce Sorensen, who had moved into the house the year before, finally gave up on it. A few years later, as a city employee, he was the one shoveling the flood's mud off the tennis courts.

1993July 23

The Flood of 1993 Hits Weeping Water

The wettest July on record, a new record crest, and a 150-year-old oak on a farmhouse roof.

65,100cfs at Union on July 23, the highest flow ever recorded there (USGS)
23 ftcrest at Weeping Water, where flood stage is 11–12 ft
3 ftthe creek rose in one hour
18.87 inof rain in Weeping Water in July, 15.12 above normal

By July 1993 the whole Midwest was flooding, and on July 23 it reached Weeping Water. About seven inches fell between midnight and 4 a.m. The creek rose three feet in an hour and crested at 23 feet just after dawn. Gospel Run backed up at the west end of Main Street, the fire hall basement filled with five feet of water, and the city shop was “ripped” and “twisted” while workers stood in three feet of water getting the snowplow out. The west bridge was closed from 1:30 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. Part of Swedetown was evacuated.

South of the Beaver Lake area, a 150-year-old oak listed as a nationally registered tree fell onto Dale and Nancy Nutzman's farmhouse. The swinging bridge survived again, though both of its approaches were twisted. That October Dale Nielsen's crew rebuilt it, with Hank Friesel, one of its original builders, acting as “chief engineer.”

Afterward came FEMA, flood-insurance forms, and new county rules: a building in the floodway that suffered 50 percent damage had to be removed, and one in the floodplain had to be raised.

1998–nowAfter the dams

Waterball, Anyone?

The creek still floods, and people still joke about it.

In June 1998 a photo of the flooded basketball courts ran under the caption Waterball anyone?, and the National Weather Service blamed El Niño. Heavy rain in June 1999 washed out a Union Pacific track at Union and closed Dewey Drive east of town.

The last big flood came on June 21, 2010, when the creek reached 48,200 cfs at Union, the fourth highest on record. The Lincoln Journal Star photographed families cleaning mud out of their basements, and one couple got out by swimming through a basement window. Since then the creek has stayed well below those levels. In 2024 the city and the NRD began designing a project to stabilize the channel along City Park, where it has been moving north into the park.

Today the Plattsmouth Journal's “Years Ago” columns retell the old floods. Families still talk about them, and the creek is usually back to its rustling.

The Case of the Mud Volleyball Game

Local memory says someone played mud volleyball after one of the floods. We haven't found it in print yet.

We searched every scanned page of the Weeping Water Republican (1882–1986) and the Plattsmouth Journal (1986–2000) for mud volleyball, mud games and post-flood cleanups. Here is what we found:

  • In June 1987, a month after the May flood, Louisville's Platte River Day included a mud volleyball game. The Louisville columnists named Judy Root “most valuable player” and asked Nancy Briggs how she managed “so much slipping and sliding and going k-r-r-r-plop.”
  • The Plattsmouth Jaycees ran an annual Mud Drags and Mud Volleyball day in the 1980s.
  • Weeping Water's own Limestone/Independence Day, started in 1988, had mud volleyball in both of its first two years (1988 and 1989), along with kids' games, fireworks and a street dance.

None of these ties a game directly to a Weeping Water Creek flood. The story may come from after 2000, when the archive ends (2010 is a good candidate), or it may never have been written down. If you were there, or know who was, please tell us.

Sources & Method

This history was built from the full-text OCR of 42,861 pages of the Weeping Water Republican (1886–1986). We searched them with OCR-tolerant patterns for flooding and its synonyms (high water, overflow, out of its banks, cloudburst, washout, freshet, torrent, “the creek rose,” bridges washed out, and many misspellings such as fiood and fl0od), along with flood-control terms such as watershed, dam, channel, riprap and Natural Resources District. The 15,808 hits were ranked by how close they fell to “creek” and local place names. Every article quoted here was then read directly from the page image and the transcription corrected by hand, because the OCR's column boundaries often run articles together. Missing issues and the Plattsmouth Journal (1986–2000) were searched in the library's online archive.

Clippings were cropped from the scans and cleaned up: uneven lighting flattened, noise removed, and the newsprint tone restored. Most Republican photographs survive only as 1-bit (pure black-and-white) scans, which lose much of the original halftone detail. We softened the dot pattern but did not use AI to repaint the photographs, so what you see is what survives. Photos from the 1990s Journal were scanned in grayscale and reproduce much better.
